# Teaching Guide: Boom or Bust?

A guide for instructors using the Boom or Bust? exercise in courses on innovation management, technology strategy, entrepreneurship, and related subjects.

---

## Exercise Formats

### Option 1: In-Class Session Opener (15–20 minutes)

**Setup (2 minutes)**
- Project the game on screen or have students open on their devices
- Brief introduction: "Every innovation you'll see won a prestigious award from expert judges. Your job is to predict which became commercial successes."

**Gameplay (8–10 minutes)**
- Students play individually or in pairs
- 10 cases at 30 seconds each
- Encourage quick gut reactions rather than overthinking

**Quick Debrief (5–8 minutes)**
- Ask for a show of hands: "Who got 7 or more correct?" (typically very few)
- Use this to launch into the day's topic on innovation uncertainty, technology commercialisation, or picking winners

**Works well before lectures on:**
- Innovation uncertainty and forecasting
- Technology adoption and diffusion
- Venture capital and startup investing
- R&D portfolio management
- Corporate innovation strategy

---

### Option 2: Pre-Class Preparation

**Student Instructions:**
> Before class, play through Boom or Bust? at [URL]. Take note of:
> 1. Your final score
> 2. One case that surprised you
> 3. One pattern you noticed across the cases
> 
> Come to class prepared to discuss what made prediction difficult.

**In-Class Follow-up (10–15 minutes)**
- Quick poll of scores (anonymously if preferred)
- Small group discussion: share surprising cases
- Whole class: what patterns emerged?

**Advantages:**
- Saves class time
- Students arrive already primed for discussion
- Creates shared reference points for the session

---

### Option 3: Extended Workshop (45–60 minutes)

**Round 1: Individual Play (10 minutes)**
- Students play the full game individually
- Record their scores

**Round 2: Team Replay (15 minutes)**
- Form teams of 3–4 students
- Teams play together, discussing each case before deciding
- Do teams outperform individuals?

**Analysis & Discussion (20–30 minutes)**
- Compare individual vs team scores
- Discuss: Did diverse perspectives help or create confusion?
- Connect to literature on expert judgment, group decision-making, and forecasting

---

## Debrief Discussion Questions

### Opening Questions
- What was your score? Were you surprised?
- Which case surprised you most? Why?
- Did you notice any patterns in what succeeded vs failed?

### Deeper Analysis
- Why do you think expert judges—who selected these as "Best of What's New"—couldn't predict commercial success?
- What factors beyond technical excellence determined outcomes?
- Several failures had major corporate backing (Google, Amazon, Microsoft). Why didn't resources guarantee success?
- Some successes came from companies with recent failures (Nintendo Switch after Wii U). What does this tell us?

### Connecting to Theory
- How does this relate to the concept of "technology push" vs "market pull"?
- What role did timing play in these outcomes?
- How might you apply real options thinking to these innovation decisions?
- What does this suggest about the value of expert forecasts in innovation?

### Practical Application
- If you were a VC seeing these innovations at launch, how would you have decided?
- How should companies manage innovation portfolios given this uncertainty?
- What due diligence would have helped distinguish winners from losers?
- Should firms aim to "pick winners" or pursue other strategies?

## Student Exercise: Find Your Own Case

Have students research and propose new cases for the game database. This reinforces learning while potentially expanding the exercise.

### Assignment Brief

> **Find a Boom or Bust**
> 
> Identify an innovation from 2010–2020 that received significant expert praise or media attention at launch, then either succeeded commercially or failed.
> 
> **Deliverable:** A case card following this format:
> 
> - **Year:** [Launch year]
> - **Name:** [Descriptive name—don't reveal the company]
> - **Description:** [2–3 sentences describing what it does, written as it would have appeared at launch. No hindsight.]
> - **Expert Quote:** [Brief quote from contemporary coverage with source]
> - **Outcome:** Boom or Bust
> - **Company:** [Revealed after prediction]
> - **Explanation:** [Why it succeeded/failed—2–3 sentences]
> - **Lesson:** [One-sentence takeaway]
> - **Sources:** [Links to contemporary coverage and outcome evidence]
> 
> **Criteria for good cases:**
> - Clear expert endorsement at the time (award, major publication praise, industry recognition)
> - Unambiguous outcome (definite commercial success or clear failure)
> - Surprising or counterintuitive in some way
> - Not already in the game

### Grading Rubric (if assessed)

| Criterion | Excellent | Satisfactory | Needs Work |
|-----------|-----------|--------------|------------|
| Expert endorsement | Clear, credible contemporary praise | Some positive coverage | Weak or retrospective |
| Outcome clarity | Unambiguous success/failure | Mostly clear | Debatable |
| Description quality | Neutral, launch-era perspective | Minor hindsight leakage | Gives away outcome |
| Lesson insight | Novel, well-articulated | Reasonable | Generic or obvious |
| Sources | Multiple credible sources | Adequate sourcing | Poorly sourced |

### Example Submission

> **Year:** 2013  
> **Name:** Smart Wristband for Gestures  
> **Description:** A wristband that reads electrical signals from arm muscles to control computers through hand gestures. Wave to answer calls, make a fist to play music. No buttons or touchscreens needed.  
> **Expert Quote:** "Like having the Force." — Wired, 2013  
> **Outcome:** Bust  
> **Company:** Thalmic Labs Myo  
> **Explanation:** Despite $120M in funding and developer enthusiasm, the gesture vocabulary was too limited for practical use. Acquired by North (later Google) in 2019; product discontinued.  
> **Lesson:** Cool demos don't equal daily utility. Users need compelling use cases, not just impressive technology.  
> **Sources:** [Wired coverage], [TechCrunch funding], [Acquisition news]

## Tips for Instructors

**Managing expectations:**
- Most students score 4–6 out of 10 (near chance)
- This is the point—use low scores to illustrate forecasting difficulty
- Avoid making students feel bad about scores; frame as "the experts couldn't do it either"

**Technical setup:**
- Game works offline—download in advance if WiFi is unreliable
- Single HTML file can be distributed via LMS if needed
- Works on phones, tablets, and laptops

**Adapting difficulty:**
- For shorter sessions, students can quit after 5 cases
- For deeper analysis, have students replay and track which categories they find easier/harder

**Common student observations to build on:**
- "The ones with big companies behind them seemed to fail more"
- "Simple ideas did better than complex ones"
- "Timing seemed really important"
- "I kept second-guessing myself"
